September 1991. Two hikers are making their way down from a mountain pass in the Alps, right on the border between Austria and Italy, when one of them spots something brown sticking out of the ice. For the first ten years, the story everyone told was simple and kind of sad. A man got caught in a storm high in the mountains, lay down, and froze to death. The ice preserved him, along with almost everything he was carrying. And what he was carrying is incredible, because nothing like it had ever survived from that time. He wore leggings and a coat made from the hides of sheep and goats. A bearskin cap. Shoes with bearskin soles, deer-hide uppers, and grass stuffed inside for warmth. He carried a flint dagger with a little woven grass sheath. He had birch-bark containers, and one of them seems to have been used to carry glowing embers, wrapped in maple leaves, so he could start a fire later. He had pieces of a fungus threaded onto leather strips, a fungus that researchers think he may have used as a kind of medicine. This wasn't some random person. This was a man prepared for the mountains.

But some of his gear was weird. He had a longbow made of yew, taller than he was. But it wasn't finished. It hadn't even been notched for a string. And his quiver held fourteen arrows. Only two of them were ready to shoot. The rest were unfinished. So here's a question to hold on to: why would a man walk up into the high mountains with a bow he couldn't use?

Then, in 2001, a radiologist named Paul Gostner was looking at X-rays of Ötzi's body, and he noticed a small shape in the left shoulder that nobody had picked up before. It was a flint arrowhead. Ötzi had been shot from behind. Later studies showed the arrow had torn through a major artery under his collarbone. With that kind of wound, high up on a mountain, five thousand years before hospitals, he probably bled to death within minutes. He didn't freeze. He was killed. And here's a detail that still gives me chills. The arrowhead was still inside him. But the arrow's shaft was never found. Some researchers think the shooter walked up and pulled it out. We can't be sure. But think about why someone might do that.

So now we have a murder. And like any murder, the question is: what happened in his last hours? Amazingly, his body can tell us. Scientists found pollen inside him, including pollen from a tree called hop-hornbeam that flowers in spring and early summer. So he most likely died around that time of year. Other pollen and evidence suggest he'd been moving between the valleys and the high mountains in his final day or two, going down, then back up again.

And then there's his last meal. In 2018, researchers carefully analysed his stomach, and it was full. He'd eaten meat from an ibex, a wild mountain goat, along with a lot of fat, and einkorn, one of the earliest farmed types of wheat. He'd eaten it not long before he died. Think about that. You don't sit down for a big, fatty meal when you're running for your life. This was a man who thought he was safe. Who thought he'd got away. From what, though?

Because there's one more injury. On his right hand, between the thumb and the index finger, there's a deep cut. It goes almost to the bone. But here's the thing: it had already started to heal. That wound was a few days old. Some experts think it's a defensive wound, the kind you get grabbing at a blade. So a few days before he died, Ötzi may have been in a fight. He survived it. He climbed up into the mountains. He ate a big meal, feeling safe. And then someone shot him in the back. And remember that unfinished bow and those unfinished arrows? Some researchers think they fit this story too. If he'd lost or broken his weapons in that earlier fight, he may have been making new ones on the move, and he simply ran out of time. It's a theory, but it would explain a lot.

And by the way, Ötzi's body has told us a lot about who he was, too. He was around forty-five years old, about one metre sixty tall. His knees and back were worn, and many of his more than sixty tattoos, simple lines and crosses made by rubbing charcoal into small cuts, sit right over those painful joints. Some researchers think they may have been an early form of pain treatment. His DNA shows his ancestors were mostly early farmers whose families had originally come from Anatolia, in what's now Turkey. A 2023 study suggests he had darker skin than the old reconstructions showed, and he was probably going bald. He was a real person. Aches, pains, and all.

Detective Horn's reading of the evidence was this. The arrow came from behind, probably from some distance away. Ötzi likely didn't see the shooter coming. He had relaxed. He'd eaten. So whoever did this probably followed him up the mountain, waited for the right moment, and took one shot. And then, remember that missing arrow shaft? Pulling it out could have been a way of removing evidence, since an arrow could be recognised. Again, that part is a theory. But here's the clue that convinced almost everyone that this was personal.

Remember the axe that made the archaeologist say "four thousand years"? It was lying right there next to the body. And it wasn't just any axe. Its blade was made of almost pure copper, fixed to a yew handle with birch tar and tight leather strips. Five thousand years ago, a copper axe was rare. It was valuable. It was a status symbol, something that said "I'm somebody important". Scientists who analysed the metal even think the copper came from as far away as southern Tuscany, hundreds of kilometres to the south. A thief would have grabbed that axe in a heartbeat. But the killer didn't take it. They left the single most valuable thing Ötzi owned, lying in the snow, right next to him.

So this almost certainly wasn't a robbery. Somebody wanted Ötzi dead, not his stuff. Maybe it was about that fight a few days earlier. Maybe it was a feud between families or communities. Maybe it was revenge. Maybe taking the axe would have been too dangerous, because everyone would know whose it was. We'll probably never know the killer's name. But we know more about the last hours of this man than we do about almost anyone else who lived five thousand years ago.
